Carbs are always the first to go. They shouldn’t be.

The scale drops fast when the bread goes – and you should know most of that first drop is water. Are carbs really the enemy? Find out how much carbs your body actually wants.

Eileen Keh, MN.Nutr23 November 20264 min read

It’s Monday. New plan. The bread goes first, then the rice, then that sneaky afternoon snack. By Thursday the scale is down two kilos and you’re a believer – carbs were the problem all along.

It’s the opening move of almost every diet. Ask a friend who is “being good this week” what they’ve cut, and I’d bet my morning matcha it’s carbs. Ask another what you should do if you want to lose some weight. They’d probably ask you to cut carbs. So why is it always the first move – and why does it seem to work so well?

Don’t get me wrong. Cutting carbs isn’t nonsense. It DOES lead to weight loss. In one review that pooled 11 weight loss trials that ran for six months or more, people on low-carb diets lost about 2.2kg more than people on low fat diets. One of the reasons low-carb diets work is because it’s simple – there is only one rule, and one rule is far easier to follow than counting anything. Low-carb plates also tend to end up heavier on protein, which maintains satiety (keeps you fuller for longer).

So yes, low-carb works. The question is why. And plot twist – it isn’t because carbs “make you fat”.

What’s actually happening on the scales? Your body stores carbohydrate as glycogen, tucked away in your liver and muscles as ready-to-use fuel. Glycogen doesn’t travel light. It’s stored with water – roughly three grams of water for every gram of glycogen.

Cut your carbs hard and you run those stores down. The water leaves with them. So those two kilos by Thursday? A big part of that is your body emptying its fuel tank, not burning fat. Eat carbs again and the tank refills, the number creeps back up – and that isn’t fat coming back either.

So, do carbs “make you fat”? No. Over the longer run, low-carb diets do come out a little ahead mostly because people end up eating fewer calories overall. In some of those same weight loss trials, people on low-carb diets cut their calories about as much as the low-fat group did. Even though nobody asked them to. The extra protein likely kept them fuller. Take a whole food group off the table and, funnily enough, you eat less food. That’s not magic. It’s maths.

How you define “low-carb” changes things too. In another review, participants adjusted their carb intake to anything up to 45% of their total calorie intake and their difference in weight loss compared to the low-fat group was only about 1kg – too small to call it a win for low-carb.

“Carbs are the enemy” has had a very long run for an idea that boils down to “eating less helps you lose weight”.

“But surely cutting carbs is the sensible place to start?” Here’s the catch. Carbs are where most of your fibre lives – oats, beans, wholegrains, fruit, vegetables. Remember the fibre article? WHO (World Health Organisation) recommends at least 25g of fibre a day for adults. Ditch the carbs and that target gets a LOT harder to hit. You lose the bread, and your gut bacteria lose their favourite food with it.

So how much carbs should you actually eat? Again, there isn’t a definite number – even WHO doesn’t set a carb target. Its view is that your carbs allowance should follow from your protein and fat – and that’s how I work it out too. Protein first, then fat, and carbs fill whatever’s left of your daily calories. Get your calories and protein right and the carbs mostly fall into place.

As a rough guide, health-focused ranges sit around 1 to 4g of carbs per kilo of body weight a day, and the more you move, the more you need. Someone who spends most of the day sitting might land near 2g/kg. Someone training hard most days could need 4g to 8g/kg.

Say you weigh 65kg and work at a desk. That puts you around 130g of carbs a day – and the more you move, the further along the scale you go.

Where you land depends on how active you are and what you enjoy eating.

Take for example a woman on a weight-loss plan who has been given a daily allowance of 1,550 calories a day. This is 500 calories less than what she burns. She still had 134g of carbs a day in her plan! A fat-loss plan. Carbs still very much on the plate.

At the end of the day, carbs go first because the scale rewards it fast. Try to remember that the first drop is mostly water. Carbs don’t “make you fat”. Eating more than you need does, wherever those calories come from. Yes, even protein.

If you want to eat less, start with your portions, not a whole food group. Keep the carbs that bring the fibre. And the rice? Put it back on the plate.

This article is general education for healthy adults, not personal medical advice. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant or on medication, or you’re about to make a big change to how you eat or train, check with your doctor first.
