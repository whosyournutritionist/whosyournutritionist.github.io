It’s not the taste. Ultra-processed foods just go down faster.

What ‘ultra-processed’ actually means, why it’s so easy to eat more of it – and why how you chew might matter more than how processed it is.

Eileen Keh, MN.Nutr28 November 20264 min read

It’s on every other headline. Ultra-processed food (UPF) is wrecking your gut, your waistline, your kids. A friend has started reading ingredient lists out loud in the supermarket (guilty!), and now you’re side-eyeing the loaf of bread in your shopping cart… So what does ‘ultra-processed’ actually mean – and should you be fretting over adding pantry staples like tinned chickpeas into your salads and curries?

Researchers sort food into four groups by how much it’s been processed, using a system called NOVA (it isn’t short for anything).

Here’s what surprises people: ‘processed’ isn’t the problem on its own. A simple loaf made from flour, water, salt and yeast is processed. So is cheese, and so is a tin of chickpeas in water and salt. Only Group 4 earns the ‘ultra’, and that comes down to what’s on the ingredient list. Even the experts can’t agree whether Group 4 is a useful box – the system has been criticised as too vague to build advice on, and the argument is still going, so I guess we have to stay tuned…

Now, the UPF study many people are referencing. In 2019, researchers at the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) had 20 adults live on a research ward for four weeks: two weeks on an ultra-processed diet, two weeks on an unprocessed one. The menus were planned so both diets offered the same calories, sugar, fat, salt and fibre content.

For example, one ultra-processed breakfast was Honey Nut Cheerios with milk and a blueberry muffin with margarine; one unprocessed breakfast was a Greek yoghurt parfait with strawberries, banana and walnuts, with apple slices on the side. Very different foods, same numbers on paper – and participants could eat as much or as little as they liked.

On the ultra-processed diet, they ate about 500 calories a day more and put on about 0.9kg in two weeks. On the unprocessed diet, the participants actually lost around 0.9kg during the two weeks.

And the part that never makes the headline? It was not the taste. People rated both diets as equally pleasant and equally familiar. They didn’t feel any hungrier, either. They just ate more.

So what was going on? How are they eating more on the ultra-processed meals when they didn’t feel hungrier?

Speed, mostly. On the ultra-processed diet, people got through their meals noticeably faster – and the ones who sped up the most tended to eat the most. The researchers’ best guess is texture: softer food that’s easier to chew and swallow goes down before your ‘I’m full’ signals have had a chance to catch up.

A smaller study in 2023 put that guess to the test. Every meal had the same calories per bite, but some were hard and chewy and some were soft – and some were ultra-processed while others weren’t. On the chewy days, people ate about 570 fewer calories a day. Whether the food was ultra-processed made no difference. Good to note here that this particular study only had 18 participants, so hold it loosely. But, it’s a strong hint that your jaw has more say than the label.

One more thing from the 2019 study: the ultra-processed food packed more calories into every bite. Eat faster and get more per mouthful, and it adds up quickly, which is likely how the weight crept on.

Real life, of course, is messier. The study was 20 people, for two weeks, with free meals cooked for them. A lot of the scarier headlines come from studies that simply watch what people eat and what happens to them – and those can’t easily separate the food from everything else going on, like money and time. Ultra-processed food is cheaper and quicker – not everyone has time to prepare home-cooked meals with whole foods.

So what do you do with all this? No fear, and no food guilt.

Pick foods you have to chew, at least some of the time. Add veggies to the easy meals – frozen peas with the convenience meals, extra peppers on the pizza. Don’t bin the supermarket bread or the cereal on principle.

A loaf made with just flour, water, yeast and salt is only ‘processed’, and plain oats barely count as processed at all. It’s the versions with flavourings, colours or emulsifiers on the label that tip them into Group 4 – so a quick look at the ingredient list tells you which is which. Don’t forget that wholegrain bread and high-fibre cereal bring fibre with them.

And homemade isn’t automatically the answer: a lasagne or a tub of homemade ice cream can be just as soft and calorie-packed.

You don’t need a perfect shopping cart. You need a few foods that make you slow down. And chew.

This article is general education for healthy adults, not personal medical advice. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant or on medication, or you’re about to make a big change to how you eat or train, check with your doctor first.
